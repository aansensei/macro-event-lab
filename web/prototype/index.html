    Methodology and Point-in-Time Architecture

    Technical specification for probability extraction, percentile interpolation, and bitemporal vintage indexing.

    Macro Event Lab constructs an hourly point-in-time record of market expectations prior to major US macroeconomic releases and pairs each distribution with the exact first print published at the release timestamp.

    1. Deriving bin probabilities from threshold contracts

    Prediction markets on Kalshi list binary threshold contracts of the form "CPI month-over-month above k%" for an ordered ladder of strikes k_1 < k_2 < ... < k_m. Let M(k_i, t) denote the mid-market price (average of best bid and best ask, scaled to [0, 1]) at hourly snapshot t.

    Because each threshold contract pays 1 if the realized print exceeds k_i, the survival function is S(k_i) = P(X > k_i). Interior bin probabilities are differences of adjacent thresholds:

    P(k_{i-1} < X <= k_i) = S(k_{i-1}) − S(k_i)

    2. Normalization and monotonicity enforcement

    Orderbook bid-ask bounce or thin liquidity in tail strikes can cause raw differences to violate monotonicity or sum to slightly more or less than 100%. We apply isotonic regression (pool-adjacent-violators) to enforce S(k_1) >= S(k_2) >= ... >= S(k_m), clip negative bin diffs to zero, and normalize bin masses p_j = raw_j / sum(raw) so the distribution sums to exactly 1.00. Both Raw and Normalized series are preserved in the threshold toggle.

    3. Computing moments, percentiles, and surprise

    From the normalized bin centers x_j and probabilities p_j, we compute the implied mean mu = sum(x_j * p_j) and standard deviation sigma = sqrt(sum((x_j − mu)^2 * p_j)). The 5th, 25th, 50th, 75th, and 95th percentiles are computed by piecewise-linear interpolation of the cumulative distribution function across bin boundaries.

    Standardized surprise is evaluated strictly against the T−1h expectation snapshot and the unrevised first print:

    Surprise (SD) = (First_Print − Mean_{T-1h}) / SD_{T-1h}

    4. Point-in-time semantics and data sources

    Macroeconomic series are frequently revised months or years after initial publication. Backtesting or evaluating market efficiency against revised data introduces look-ahead bias. Every observation in Macro Event Lab carries two timestamps: reference_period (the month measured) and known_from (the exact timestamp when the value became publicly available in ALFRED or Kalshi).

    Data sources: (1) Kalshi prediction market limit orderbook snapshots (KXCPI, KXNFP, KXFOMC); (2) Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis ALFRED archival vintages (CPIAUCSL, PAYEMS, DFEDTARU); (3) FRED daily Treasury constant maturity yields (DGS2, DGS10).

    Note 1: Why T−1h is the benchmark. CPI and Employment Situation reports are published at 08:30 ET. Using the 07:30 ET (T−1h) snapshot captures final pre-release positioning before illiquidity widens spreads in the final seconds.
    Note 2: Discrete FOMC outcomes. Unlike continuous CPI bins, FOMC decisions fall into ordered policy actions (Cut 50, Cut 25, Hold, Hike 25). We render FOMC expectations with a 100% stacked bar and stacked area chart in ordered blues rather than a continuous fan.
    Note 3: Government shutdowns. When a release is cancelled or indefinitely delayed (such as October 2025 CPI), the pre-release market snapshots remain queryable while first print and surprise fields remain null.

    About Macro Event Lab
    
      
    Macro Event Lab shows what prediction markets expected before each US CPI report, jobs report and Fed decision, how that compared with the number first published, and how markets reacted.
    

      
    
        
    Why it exists
    
        
    
          
    Numbers as they were known
    Official figures get revised. Judging a forecast against today's revised number uses information nobody had on release day, so every value here carries the moment it became public, and every chart can be replayed hour by hour.
    
          
    The whole distribution
    A prediction market prices every outcome, not one consensus number. That shows how sure the market was, how that certainty changed in the week before a release, and how large the surprise really was.
    
          
    An engineering project
    Behind the site is an automated, tested data pipeline that collects public market and economic data and rebuilds every table from the raw responses it saved.
    
        
    
      
    

      
    
        
    How it is built
    
        
    
          
    Data sources
    Kalshi public market data for the "above k" contracts on CPI, payrolls, GDP and the Fed rate; ALFRED vintages from the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis for first prints and revisions; FRED release calendars; the Federal Reserve FOMC calendar.
    
          
    Pipeline
    Python with httpx, Polars, DuckDB and Parquet. Every API response is stored first as compressed JSON named by its hash, then parsed into clean tables that can be rebuilt from scratch at any time.
    
          
    Methods
    As-of joins so a snapshot only sees prices known at that hour; weighted isotonic regression to turn contract prices into a consistent probability curve; bins aligned with how each number is published, so a CPI bin is centred on the value it can actually print.
    
          
    Quality
    pytest with Hypothesis property tests, including one that checks no snapshot ever uses later data; ruff; data quality checks on every build; continuous integration on GitHub Actions.
    
          
    Website
    This prototype is plain HTML, CSS and JavaScript with hand-drawn SVG charts. The production site is planned in React, TypeScript and Vite with d3, loading DuckDB-WASM only on the SQL page.
    
          
    Hosting
    Planned as a static site at no cost.

    FAQ
    
        
    
          
    
            What does "point in time" mean here?
            
    Each number is shown as it was known at a chosen moment. Drag the time slider to 48 hours before a release and the page shows only the market prices and official figures that were public at that hour, never anything published later.
    
          
    
          
    
            Why prediction markets instead of economist surveys?
            
    Markets trade around the clock and price every possible outcome, so their expectations can be read hour by hour as a full distribution. A survey gives one median number, collected days before the release.
    
          
    
          
    
            Why compare with the first print and not today's number?
            
    On release day the market reacted to the first published number. Revisions arrive weeks or months later; they are shown separately, so the comparison stays fair to what was known at the time.
    
          
    
          
    
            What happens when a release is not published?
            
    Some releases never came out, such as October 2025 CPI during the government shutdown. The market data is still shown; the first print and the surprise stay empty instead of being filled in.
    
          
    
          
    
            Are these numbers real?
            
    Not in this prototype: it uses illustrative data to test the design. The pipeline behind it runs on real data, and the public site will show derived tables only, never raw market data.
    
          
    
          
    
            Can I download the data?
            
    Every chart has a table view and a CSV download of exactly the rows it draws.
