## 2. The mechanism

The chain of reasoning from theory to this number.

Macro. A price index rises when firms can pass higher costs to buyers, and how much they can pass depends on demand. When demand is steady, price changes from month to month are small and mostly noise around a trend. Over the last twelve months the first prints ranged from minus 0.4 to plus 0.9, a wide spread for a trend that moves slowly. The trend of the last year is about 0.3 to 0.35 a month, so the best guess for one coming month is near that, and a single high month such as 0.4 or 0.6 is more likely to be followed by something nearer the average than by something higher. The models that lean on the longer record (autoregression, ridge, LASSO) say 0.24 to 0.26. The naive forecast follows the last print and says 0.4.

Micro. The monthly figure is built from the prices of a large basket collected across the month. Housing costs are the largest part and change slowly, so most of the month to month movement comes from the volatile parts, mainly fuel and food. A seasonal adjustment smooths regular patterns but not shocks. The first print is published in tenths of a percentage point, so a true change of 0.34 and one of 0.26 are reported as 0.3, and a miss of one tenth can be only rounding. Because the figure is almost never revised in the next report, the risk with this number is not that it will be edited later but that a forecast can miss it on the day.

## 3. My prediction

A first print for November of 0.3 percent, in a range from 0.0 to 0.6.

Direction against the models: at or slightly above the four models (0.24 to 0.32), and below the naive forecast (0.4).

Conditional statements:

- If the average retail price of gasoline in November rose by more than about 3 percent over October, the print is at 0.4 or above. Fuel is a few percent of the basket, so a move of that size shifts the index by roughly a tenth of a point.
- If the average retail price of gasoline in November fell by more than about 3 percent over October, the print is at 0.2 or below.
- If gasoline moved by less than 3 percent either way, the print is at 0.3, the middle of the last year.

## 4. What would change my mind

- A sharp swing in energy or food prices late in the month that the average gasoline price does not show.
- A change in how the index is measured or a gap in data collection that moves the number without moving prices.
- A revision of the October figure in the same report. The next report almost never changes a month, so a revision would suggest that the recent trend is stronger than the first prints showed, and it would move my centre toward the naive forecast.
- Evidence that the models that follow the last print (naive, ridge on principal components) have been closer in recent months. In the last twelve months the errors of all five were between 0.30 and 0.38 in root mean squared terms, which is too close to separate on twelve observations.
